Hydrostatic pressure exists because lower layers of fluid must support the weight of the layers above them. In free fall, every element of water accelerates downward at $g$ simultaneously. Consequently, there is no contact force or compression between adjacent vertical layers of water—the effective gravity inside the container is zero.

**Step-by-Step Solution:**

1. **Effective Gravity in a Free-Falling Reference Frame:**
    
    Working in the non-inertial reference frame of the falling container, an upward inertial (pseudo) force of magnitude $\rho g$ per unit volume cancels gravity:
    
    $$g_{\text{eff}} = g - a = g - g = 0$$
    
2. **Internal Pressure Field:**
    
    The hydrostatic pressure gradient is:
    
    $$\frac{dP}{dz} = -\rho g_{\text{eff}} = 0$$
    
    This means pressure is uniform throughout the entire liquid. Because the container is open to the atmosphere at the top, the pressure at the free surface is $P_{\text{atm}}$. Therefore, at every point inside the liquid:
    
    $$P_{\text{inside}} = P_{\text{atm}}$$

# Pressure ( also Force = PA ) on Side walls of a container

The general method to remember

For any hydrostatic force question involving a vertical wall:

1. Find the pressure at the top and bottom of each section of the wall.
    
2. If pressure varies linearly, calculate its average using $$(\frac{P_{\text{top}}+P_{\text{bottom}}}{2})$$
    
3. Multiply average pressure by that section's area.
    
4. Add the forces from all sections.
    

The crucial distinction is that pressure at a point depends on the liquid above that point, but force on a wall depends on the pressure distribution over its entire area.

## The missing formula

$$\boxed{Q=\frac{\pi r^4\Delta P}{8\eta L}}$$

This is Poiseuille's law, for steady laminar flow of a viscous liquid through a cylindrical tube.

- \(Q\): volume of liquid flowing per second
    
- \(r\): tube radius
    
- \(\Delta P\): pressure difference across the tube
    
- \(\eta\): liquid's viscosity
    
- \(L\): tube length

### The rule to remember

|Situation|Increase in scale reading|
|---|---|
|Fully submerged, held by an external support|Weight of displaced liquid|
|Fully submerged, resting on the tank bottom|Object's full weight|
|Floating freely|Object's full weight|

The underlying principle is simple: if the object is supported entirely by the tank-water system, the scale ultimately bears its full weight. If an external support carries some of the load, the scale doesn't have to.
